Eliminating AMA lobbying, lifting federal residency caps, and allowing an unlimited supply of doctors would fundamentally reshape the economics of U.S. healthcare, potentially driving down total healthcare costs by an estimated 15% to 25% over a decade.

In a completely deregulated, free-market physician supply system, healthcare expenditures would decline through a combination of lower physician salaries, reduced emergency room utilization, the elimination of scope-of-practice restrictions, and minimized administrative overhead.

Breaking the Cartel: How Flooding the Market with Doctors Would Slash Healthcare Costs

The United States currently spends roughly 17.5% of its GDP on healthcare, drastically outspending every other developed nation. While multiple factors drive these bloated figures, an artificial scarcity of physicians sits at the very center of the crisis. For decades, the American Medical Association (AMA) and federal lawmakers have bottlenecked the supply of doctors through artificial residency caps established by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997.

If the AMA's lobbying influence were completely removed, federal funding caps eliminated, and an unlimited supply of doctors allowed to enter the market, the financial relief to American consumers and taxpayers would be historic.

1. Market Competition and Normalized Physician Salaries

  • The Cost Driver: U.S. doctors are paid significantly more than their international peers, with specialists routinely clearing $500,000 annually. This is a direct consequence of a restricted labor supply.
  • The Free-Market Shift: Flooding the market with qualified domestic and international medical graduates would instantly trigger standard supply-and-demand economics. As doctors compete for patients, the nominal fees charged for clinical visits and procedures would face downward pressure.
  • Estimated Savings: Bringing American physician salaries down by 30% to align closer with European standards would directly shave billions off national expenditures. Given that physician salaries account for roughly 10% of total healthcare spending, a salary normalization would drop overall costs by 3% to 4%.

2. Defeating "Scope of Practice" Firewalls

  • The Cost Driver: A massive portion of AMA lobbying budget goes toward defeating "scope of practice" bills in state legislatures. The AMA fights to prevent Nurse Practitioners (NPs) and Physician Assistants (PAs) from opening independent practices, forcing patients to pay higher physician rates for routine care.
  • The Free-Market Shift: Stripping away the AMA's lobbying shield would allow advanced practice clinicians to operate independently nationwide. Furthermore, an abundance of fully licensed doctors would force physicians to compete directly with mid-level providers, shattering the pricing monopolies on low-risk medical care.
  • Estimated Savings: Shifting routine check-ups, basic prescriptions, and minor urgent care issues away from high-cost physician environments would slash primary care delivery costs by 15% to 20% within that specific sub-sector.

3. Ending the Expensive Cascade of Fragmented Care

  • The Cost Driver: The artificial doctor shortage creates massive wait times for primary care. When patients cannot see a regular family doctor, they wait until their conditions worsen and seek care in a hospital Emergency Room (ER). A single ER visit costs up to ten times more than a standard clinic visit.
  • The Free-Market Shift: An unlimited supply of doctors means same-day appointments, robust preventative medicine, and competitive clinic hours. Chronic diseases like diabetes and hypertension would be managed early, preventing expensive hospitalizations, redundant testing, and advanced surgeries.
  • Estimated Savings: Because hospital care represents the single largest slice of U.S. healthcare spending (roughly 30%), a massive reduction in preventable ER visits and hospital admissions would slash overall national healthcare spending by an estimated 5% to 8%.

4. Eradicating Regulatory Capture and Code Monopolies

  • The Cost Driver: The AMA wields immense administrative power through its ownership of Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes—the copyrighted system every hospital must pay royalties to use when billing insurance. Furthermore, the AMA closely advises the federal government on how to value those codes, routinely inflating the value of specialist procedures over primary care.
  • The Free-Market Shift: Eliminating the AMA's administrative monopoly would lead to open-source medical billing infrastructure and a neutral valuation of medical services.
  • Estimated Savings: De-escalating the billing bureaucracy and ending the specialist-driven code distortion would lower administrative overhead—which currently consumes 25% of physician revenue—saving an additional 2% to 3% across the entire system.

The Counter-Argument: Supplier-Induced Demand

Skeptics of total deregulation point to a theory known as supplier-induced demand. This theory suggests that if you flood a market with doctors, they will simply recommend unnecessary tests and procedures to maintain their high income levels.

However, in a truly open market devoid of AMA lobbying, price transparency would flourish. Without federal caps protecting residency slots, hospitals could hire talented doctors at highly competitive rates, and the sheer volume of choices would empower patients to shop for high-value, lower-cost healthcare.

The Bottom Line

By treating medical education and licensure as a protected guild rather than an open labor market, the U.S. system forces consumers to pay premium prices for scarce resources. Eliminating the AMA's lobbying apparatus, abolishing federal residency funding freezes, and allowing the physician supply to expand dynamically would crush the artificial scarcity. The result would be a more agile, accessible, and market-competitive system, ultimately saving American taxpayers and patients upwards of $800 billion annually.
