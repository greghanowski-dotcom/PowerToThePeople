Why the Budget Deficit Never Gets Fixed

Congress continually ignores budget deficits primarily because the short-term political rewards of spending money and cutting taxes outweigh the long-term economic risks of rising national debt.

Politicians face a strong structural incentive to focus on immediate election cycles. This creates a system where passing popular laws matters more than maintaining a balanced budget.

Short-Term Incentives vs. Long-Term Risks

The core conflict stems from a misalignment between political survival and fiscal responsibility.

  • The Election Cycle: Members of the House of Representatives face reelection every two years. This short window forces them to focus on immediate results that voters can see right away.

  • The Delayed Penalty: The economic fallout of a high deficit, such as inflation or higher interest rates, often takes years or decades to fully manifest. Politicians rarely face electoral consequences for debt that matures long after they leave office.

The Political Benefits of Deficits

Deficits are often the mathematical byproduct of policies that are highly popular with voters and special interest groups.

  • Bringing Home the Bacon: Lawmakers win votes by directing federal funding toward projects in their home districts, like roads, schools, or military bases.

  • The Popularity of Spending: Passing laws to fund programs like infrastructure, education, or healthcare creates visible benefits for constituents.

  • The Appeal of Tax Cuts: Reducing taxes leaves more money in voters' pockets, which is highly popular, even though it reduces government revenue and widens the deficit.

  • The "Starve the Beast" Strategy: Some lawmakers deliberately vote for tax cuts to limit government resources, hoping it will eventually force spending cuts. However, those spending cuts rarely follow.


 

The Punishing Cost of Fiscal Discipline

Conversely, the actions required to actually reduce a budget deficit are politically toxic.

  • The Third Rail of Politics: The vast majority of federal spending goes toward mandatory programs like Social Security and Medicare. Proposing cuts to these programs is often considered political suicide.

  • The Fear of Tax Hikes: Raising taxes to close the budget gap is deeply unpopular with voters and corporate donors, frequently leading to electoral defeat.

  • The Concentrated Cost: While a balanced budget benefits the entire nation vaguely, the pain of a spending cut or tax hike is felt sharply and immediately by specific groups of people. Those groups will actively organize and vote against the politicians responsible.

Institutional Hurdles and Economic Theories

Beyond basic politics, the rules of Congress and changing economic philosophies make balancing the budget difficult.

  • Polarization and Gridlock: Deep political divisions make it nearly impossible for both parties to agree on a compromise package of spending cuts and tax increases.

  • The Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) Influence: Some modern economic perspectives argue that nations printing their own currency can run high deficits safely, as long as inflation remains under control. This viewpoint gives politicians economic cover to ignore the debt.

  • Emergency Spending Traps: Congress routinely passes massive, bipartisan spending bills during crises, such as economic recessions or global pandemics. Once these high spending levels are established, rolling them back to baseline levels is politically difficult.

The Deficit Feedback Loop

When the government spends more than it collects in taxes, it creates a deficit. To cover that gap, the U.S. Treasury borrows money by issuing bonds and notes to investors.

  • Higher Borrowing Costs: As old government bonds mature, the Treasury must replace them by issuing new debt at current, higher market interest rates.

  • Competing for Cash: Investors only have a limited pool of cash to lend. If the government is competing with corporations, home buyers, and car shoppers for that same cash, it cannot easily find enough buyers at a low rate.

  • Credit Rating Downgrades: If a country's debt grows too fast, independent agencies may lower its credit rating. A lower credit rating legally forces many large investment funds to stay away, further shrinking the pool of buyers and forcing rates even higher to attract buyers.

  • Credit Risk: If a person or a government owes a massive amount of money relative to their income, lenders begin to worry about their ability to pay it back. To compensate for the growing risk that a heavily indebted borrower might struggle, stumble, or default on payments, lenders demand a higher interest rate.

  • Inflation: High levels of national debt often go hand-in-hand with inflation, which directly forces interest rates upward.  Lenders know that if a government prints money to handle its debt, the value of the dollar will drop. If an investor buys a 10-year bond, they want to make sure the money they get back in a decade can still buy things. To protect themselves against inflation eating away their profits, lenders demand higher interest rates upfront.

  • Exploding Net Interest Expense: The total amount of money the government spends just to pay back interest balloons. Net interest payments have crossed $1 trillion per year, making interest the second-largest single expense in the entire federal budget.

  • Compounding Debt: Because Congress does not have extra cash to cover the massive new interest payments, it must borrow more money at a higher interest rate just to pay the interest on the money it already owes. This added borrowing increases the deficit further, creating a compounding vicious cycle..

The National Debt Reduces Your Quality of Life

  • Benchmark for Consumer Loans: Government bond yields serve as the pricing floor for the rest of the financial world. When the Treasury has to pay higher rates to attract investors, it pushes up interest rates across the entire economy.

  • Squeezing Households: This economic pressure directly leads to higher borrowing costs for regular citizens, translating into more expensive mortgages, auto loans, and credit card rates.

  • Crowding Out Resources: As interest costs consume a larger share of federal revenue—already taking up over a third of all individual income taxes—less money is available for public priorities like infrastructure, defense, or education.


 

Ending the Vicious Cycle

Warren Buffett outlined his most famous plan to fix the U.S. deficit during a CNBC interview, stating:

"I could end the deficit in five minutes. You just pass a law that says that any time there is a deficit of more than 3% of GDP, all sitting members of Congress are ineligible for reelection. Yeah, now you've got the incentives in the right place, right?" 

Buffett's proposal focuses on holding lawmakers personally accountable for fiscal health, suggesting that passing legislation with strict consequences would immediately alter the incentives governing federal spending.

But we need budget surpluses to start whittling down the debt, so the following bill goes ever further.

Fiscal Responsibility and Congressional Accountability Act

To amend title 3, United States Code, and the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, to establish statutory fiscal accountability for members of Congress by disqualifying sitting members from reelection during periods of excessive federal deficits, and for other purposes.

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled,

1. FINDINGS AND PURPOSE

The purpose of this Act is to align the personal electoral incentives of members of Congress with the fiscal health of the United States by establishing immediate political consequences for structural federal deficits.

2. RE-ELECTION INELIGIBILITY FOR FISCAL RESPONSIBILITY FAILURE.

(a) Ineligibility Trigger.—Notwithstanding any other provision of law, if the total federal budget deficit for any given fiscal year exceeds the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) of the United States for that same fiscal year, all individuals who served as members of the Senate or the House of Representatives during that fiscal year shall be immediately ineligible to run for re-election or election to any seat in the Senate or the House of Representatives.

(b) Determination of Deficit and GDP

  1. Reporting Agency.—The Director of the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), in consultation with the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), shall issue a final, audited fiscal report within thirty (30) days following the end of each fiscal year.

  2. Certification.—The report must certify the exact federal surplus as a percentage of that fiscal year's nominal GDP.

(c) Enforcement by Election Authorities.—

  1. Federal Election Commission (FEC).—Upon certification that the deficit exceeded the budget, the FEC shall ban affected individuals from registering political committees or raising funds for future congressional campaigns.

  2. State Authorities.—Chief election officers of each State shall refuse to place the name of any ineligible individual on any primary, general, or special election ballot for federal office.

 

3. EXCEPTIONS AND EXEMPTIONS

(a) Declaration of War.—The provisions of Section 3 shall not apply if a formal declaration of war against a foreign nation is in effect pursuant to Article I, Section 8, Clause 11 of the United States Constitution during more than 180 days of the applicable fiscal year.
